How to leave QuickBooks for a spreadsheet (without losing your books)

QuickBooks Desktop is discontinued, Online costs $38 to $275 a month after the hikes, and the cancellation flow is famously a maze. If you run a small service business, the exit exists and it costs about $49 once, not $2,400 a year. Here is the migration, step by step, without losing a single invoice.

First: who should NOT do this

Honesty first. Stay on accounting software if any of these are true: you run payroll through it, you're above roughly 100 transactions a month, you collect sales tax in several states, or your accountant refuses to work from spreadsheets. The spreadsheet route is for the stage before that: freelancers, solo operators, and 1-20 person service businesses whose accounting needs are really just invoices, expenses, and a cash view.

Everyone else: keep reading.

Step 1. Export everything before you cancel

QuickBooks lets you export your reports and lists while the account is active. Before cancelling, pull:

  • Your customer and vendor lists (CSV from the lists menu)
  • Profit & Loss and Balance Sheet for the last two fiscal years (PDF + Excel)
  • The transaction detail by account report, date range all-time, exported to Excel. This is the master record of every invoice, payment and bill.

Do not cancel until these files are safely backed up in two places. The cancellation flow may be annoying, but the export takes twenty minutes and protects years of records.

The mistake most people make is recreating QuickBooks, all of it. You don't need double-entry bookkeeping; you need three things that answer real questions:

1. An invoice register. One row per invoice: number, client, date, due date, amount, tax, status. This answers "who owes me and how late?" - the Monday morning question.

2. An expense log. One row per payment with a category. This answers "what did I actually spend?" and feeds your tax return.

3. A 12-month cashflow view. Expected money in and out per month, so the spreadsheet warns you about the tight month before it arrives.

Our Small Business Command Center is exactly this triangle, pre-linked: quotes become invoices, invoices feed the forecast, and a dashboard shows receivables aging and client profitability. One file, $49 once, no subscription. (There is also a free 12-month cashflow forecaster if you only need the third piece.)

Step 3. Re-enter only what matters going forward

You do not need to migrate ten years of history into the new sheet. Enter open items only: unpaid invoices, unprocessed bills, current bank balance. Historical totals stay in the exported reports from step 1 - that's what the tax authority and your accountant will ask for anyway.

From day one, the habit is lighter than QuickBooks ever was: log an invoice when you send it, log an expense when it happens, ten minutes on Friday to glance at the dashboard.

Step 4. Keep the accountant happy

Two things make an accountant accept a spreadsheet business without complaint: a clean category structure that matches the tax return's expense categories, and a monthly summary they can read in one page. If your spreadsheet produces those - ours does, the categories are built to match Schedule C headings - the accountant's job is unchanged. The software brand never mattered to them.

The math, one more time

QuickBooks Online at the tiers most small service businesses get pushed into: $456 to $1,200 a year, rising. The Command Center: $49 once, lifetime updates included, works in Excel and Google Sheets. Over three years that is $1,300 to $3,500 kept, for maybe two hours of migration work.

The subscription era quietly assumed small businesses would rather pay monthly than think about their books once. A well-built spreadsheet is the polite refusal.
