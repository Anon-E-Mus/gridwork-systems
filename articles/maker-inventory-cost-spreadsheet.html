Maker inventory spreadsheet: the stock ledger that reconciles (candles, tees, and everything you make)

Materials in, production converts, sales decrement: the double-entry stock habit for makers. COGS per batch worked to the candle, reorder points with the formula — and when spreadsheets stop being enough.

The gistMaterials in, production converts, sales decrement: the double-entry stock habit for makers. COGS per batch worked to the candle, reorder points with the formula — and when spreadsheets stop being enough.

The familiar maker problem: out of the thing that sells, overstocked on the things that don't. The wax is in the garage, half of it already poured, the market cash box never got written down, and Etsy says you have three of a candle you can find one of. A craft inventory spreadsheet fixes this with one habit, not one more app. Here is the method, the layout, and the honest limits — free route included.

Why makers lose track (it is not laziness)

Three things break every maker's stock numbers:

1. Materials get bought for products, not tracked as stock. You buy 10kg of wax, forty jars and a bag of wicks "for the autumn restock" — and the moment they land, they stop being money and become garage. Nothing decremented anything.

2. Half-made batches are invisible. Twelve jars poured and curing, labels not on. Are they materials, finished stock, or cash? In most makers' heads they are a vague guilt. In a ledger they are a number.

3. Market-day sales go unlogged. Busy stall, cash box, QR codes, a friend helping. Nobody tallies what sold. You get home tired, count the money, and reconcile nothing.

Any one of these drifts your numbers; together they make them fiction within weeks. The fix is not counting more often — it is a ledger where every stock movement gets an entry, the same double-entry logic your bookkeeping uses, applied to things instead of money.

The double-entry habit for stock

Only three moves ever happen to stock, and each one touches two places:

EventMaterials ledgerFinished-goods ledger
---------
Buy materials+ qty in—
Production run− materials used+ units made
Sale (any channel)—− units sold

That is the entire method: every purchase adds materials stock, every production run converts materials into finished stock, every sale decrements finished stock. Purchases never touch finished goods; sales never touch materials; production is the bridge.

Do this consistently and the ledger only disagrees with the shelf for a reason you can name — a miscounted pour, an unlogged sale, a purchase never entered. And the two questions that matter get answers: what do I have and what is it worth (count × unit cost — the number an accountant calls inventory, and one most makers can only guess).

The materials ledger — one row per material: wax by the kilo, jars by the each, wicks by the hundred, tees by size. Every purchase adds a row and tops up quantity on hand.

The finished-goods ledger — one row per SKU; a different scent or vessel size is a different SKU, and "candles" is not a SKU. Columns for on-hand, unit cost (next section), selling price, last count date.

The production log — the bridge. One row per pour, press or sewing session: date, materials consumed, units produced. It decrements materials, increments finished goods, takes two minutes — and is the piece most makers skip.

COGS per batch — the same math as recipe costing

Batch costing is materials + labor + overhead, then divide by yield — the same math as recipe and job costing, wearing a wick. This is the tab that makes it a candle business spreadsheet, not a list of jars. A 24-candle batch:

That $5.55 is the unit cost the finished-goods ledger carries. Sell a candle and inventory drops $5.55 while revenue rises — across every SKU, "what is my stock worth" becomes a one-line formula instead of a weekend of guessing. Materials at purchase-unit prices scaled to the batch, overhead as a flat 10–20 percent: crude and vastly better than zero.

Reorder points: days of cover, not vibes

The rule: reorder when stock falls below X days of average sales. One formula per SKU:

> Average daily sales × (supplier lead time + cushion weeks × 7) = reorder point

A scent sells about 10 a week across channels — call it 1.4 a day. Wax supplier takes a week; you want two weeks of cushion. Reorder point: 1.4 × 21 ≈ 30 on hand. Hit 29, order for the next pour. Materials work the same way: that batch eats 4kg of wax, so reorder below 8kg if you always want two batches' worth. A conditional format does the watching.

One honesty note: trailing averages lie when the season turns. Use the last 60–90 days and adjust upward before craft-fair season — the answer there is usually "make more than the math says."

Why "I'll count next month" always ends badly

It fails in both directions, and both cost money:

  • Stockout. The scent that sells is gone on the busiest Saturday of the month. You lose the sale, then the momentum — a dormant Etsy listing and a hole in the table take weeks to wake up.
  • Dead cash. Shelves of what doesn't sell. Every unsold candle is wax, jar, fragrance, labor and a label, sitting perfectly still. Postpone counting for a year and it is startling how much of your money turns out to be sitting on a shelf.

The fix: a full physical count per season, compared to the ledger, differences logged as adjustment rows. Small variances are normal — breakage, testers, gifts. A big one on a single SKU is a specific leak: unlogged market sales, a yield never updated, shrinkage. A count takes an hour when the ledger is close, a weekend when it isn't — the ledger is what makes counting cheap.

Market days and Etsy: logging sales from two worlds

  • Markets: don't log per-order at the stall. Tally per-SKU on a sheet or phone, transfer it at pack-down the same evening, and count the cash box against the tally — the difference is your variance, not a mystery.
  • Etsy: don't hand-type orders. Download the order CSV and paste it into an order log tab; formulas decrement finished goods per line. (Small orders commonly lose roughly 11 percent to fees before materials.)
  • One order log, one channel column — market, Etsy, website, wholesale. That column tells you which channel actually moves stock, and which one mostly pays fees.

The honest limits of a spreadsheet

An inventory tracker for makers built in a spreadsheet is genuinely fine for a long time — commonly fine up to a few hundred SKUs (materials plus finished goods), one or two people updating, channels you can tally or paste without pain.

It stops being right when several of these arrive together: live channels overselling the same last unit; variations that have exploded (scent × vessel × size); syncing taking longer than making; several people plus a barcode scanner. That is what real inventory software (Craftybase and similar) is for — a monthly subscription that automates the double-entry and syncs channels. Below that threshold it is a fee to avoid ten minutes a day; above it, it is cheap. The honest answer is a threshold, not a team.

FAQ

How do I set a reorder point without overthinking it?

Average daily sales over the last 60–90 days, times supplier lead time plus a two-week cushion. One formula per SKU, one red conditional format. Revisit before your busy season, because a trailing average lies when demand shifts.

Should I track materials or just finished goods?

Both, in separate ledgers, connected by a production log. Finished goods tells you what you can sell today; materials tells you what you could make next week. Track only finished goods and you will rebuy wax you already own; track only materials and the gap finds you mid-market on a Saturday.

When should I stop using a spreadsheet for inventory?

Commonly when two or three of these arrive together: a few hundred SKUs, several live channels overselling the same stock, syncing slower than making, or multiple people updating at once. Below that, a spreadsheet with the double-entry habit is not a compromise — it is the right tool.
