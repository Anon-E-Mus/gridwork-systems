QuickBooks alternatives 2027: the spreadsheet-first stack for small business (honest comparison)

Desktop is dead and Enterprise costs $1,799. Seven honest alternatives ranked: plain spreadsheets with a good template, Wave, FreshBooks, Xero, Zoho Books, open source, or a bookkeeper.

Intuit killed QuickBooks Desktop, pushed everyone onto subscriptions, and hiked Enterprise from $1,199 to $1,799 a year — so a wave of small businesses is hunting for a cheap QuickBooks replacement at the same time. Here are seven alternatives, ranked spreadsheet-first, with real prices and real weaknesses. Including the free one most comparison posts bury at number nine.

What actually happened to QuickBooks

Three changes, stacked:

1. QuickBooks Desktop is sunset. Intuit ended it. The buy-it-once, own-it-for-a-decade era is over, and everyone is being moved onto subscriptions whether they like it or not.

2. The price of staying went up. Enterprise moved from $1,199 to $1,799 per year. That is a 50 percent jump for the same job.

3. The UK deadline landed. Making Tax Digital went live in April 2026 — digital records are now mandatory, not optional, and the thresholds widen again in 2027. (A spreadsheet can still comply, done right. More on that below.)

The result is predictable: users fleeing, and a search results page for "quickbooks alternative for small business" full of listicles that rank whatever pays the affiliate the most per click. This article has affiliate links in it too — the difference is we rank free and nearly-free options first, because for most small businesses they genuinely are enough. Every weakness below is real. So is every price.

The spreadsheet-first test

Spreadsheet accounting for a small business is not a compromise; it is usually the right-sized tool. Most small businesses need exactly four things from their books:

  • Know what came in and what went out
  • Know who owes you money
  • See a cash squeeze coming more than a week out
  • Hand the accountant clean records once a year

A good spreadsheet does all four for $0 a month. Every paid tool below has to earn its slot by doing something a spreadsheet cannot: live bank feeds, automatic reconciliation, payroll, tax filing. That is the honest test, and it sets the ranking.

2. Wave — free accounting that is actually free

Wave is the strongest pick on this list after the spreadsheet itself, and the one most people should try when the spreadsheet runs out of road: Wave's free plan.

Cost: the accounting core — invoicing, income and expense tracking, basic reports — is free. Wave makes its money on payroll and the paid Pro tier (around $25 a month last we checked; confirm current pricing).

Who it fits: the business that wants real double-entry books and live bank feeds without a subscription. It is the natural first upgrade from a spreadsheet, and the two stack well: Wave for the books, a spreadsheet for the forecast, because Wave's forecasting is thin.

Honest weakness: free means free-tier support — slow when something breaks. Bank feed sync can be flaky, and the feature set is strongest in North America. If your situation needs hand-holding, the paid tools below hold hands better.

3. FreshBooks — invoicing-first, built for service businesses

Cost: a monthly subscription, tiered by what your business needs, and heavy introductory discounts are the norm — check current pricing before judging the sticker: FreshBooks.

Who it fits: freelancers and service businesses that live on invoices — proposals, time tracking, and late-payment reminders are built in, not bolted on. If "getting paid" is your bottleneck, this is the tool shaped around it.

Honest weakness: it is invoicing software with accounting attached, not the other way around. Bookkeepers grumble about its double-entry depth, the price scales up as you add capability, and a simple business can end up paying for depth it never touches.

4. Xero — the accountant's favorite

Cost: monthly subscription, plan-tiered, no free tier: Xero.

Who it fits: businesses that want full double-entry with a huge app ecosystem — and UK businesses in particular, since it is widely used by UK accountants and handles the Making Tax Digital workflow natively. If your accountant has a preference, it is probably this or QuickBooks Online.

Honest weakness: subscription creep. Payroll, expenses, and half the useful extras are add-ons with their own monthly fees, so the real bill tends to grow past the headline. And it is overkill for a business that just needs to know whether it can afford Friday's payroll run.

5. Zoho Books — the quiet cheap one

Cost: a free tier for very small outfits, and paid plans starting from around $20 a month.

Who it fits: businesses already inside the Zoho suite (CRM, invoicing, everything else), and anyone who wants proper double-entry books at the lowest subscription price on this list.

Honest weakness: the interface does everything, which means the interface does everything — dense, menu-heavy, slower to learn than Wave or FreshBooks. Depth in UK and US tax specifics trails Xero. Cheap, not effortless.

6. GnuCash / Actual — open source, $0 forever

Cost: free. Genuinely free — open source, no upsell, no vendor who can hike you $600 in one announcement. GnuCash is proper double-entry accounting on the desktop; Actual is local-first and leans more toward budgeting than full books.

Who it fits: the principle-driven (your data stays on your machine), the offline crowd, and businesses that will never pay a monthly fee on principle.

Honest weakness: no bank feeds without manual file imports, a dated interface (GnuCash especially), and you are your own support desk. UK readers will need separate tooling for Making Tax Digital filings. Powerful, honest, and about as far from hand-holding as software gets.

7. A bookkeeper + shared spreadsheets — the human option

Cost: varies hugely by region and transaction volume — get three quotes. Often cheaper than owners expect once you count the hours you get back.

Who it fits: owners whose time is worth more than the fee, and anyone whose eyes glaze over at reconciliation. The setup that works: the bookkeeper keeps the formal books in their software, and you both look at a simple shared spreadsheet as the live dashboard. A human catches the weird stuff software never will — the duplicate subscription, the miscoded category, the customer who quietly started paying late.

Honest weakness: a recurring cost tied to a person, and quality varies enormously. A bad bookkeeper is worse than no bookkeeper. Vet them.
