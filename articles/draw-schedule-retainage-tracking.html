Draw schedule and retainage tracking: the spreadsheet way

Finished work that never got billed, retainage nobody is tracking: the two quiet leaks in contracting cash. Five columns fix both.

Pricing & Job Costing4 min readGridwork Systems · Nov 2026
The gistFinished work that never got billed, retainage nobody is tracking: the two quiet leaks in contracting cash. Five columns fix both.

Progress billing should be the happiest part of a construction job: the work is done, the invoice goes out, the money comes in. Instead, most small contractors run it from memory, and the memory leaks. Draws get finished but never billed, retainage evaporates from attention, and the gap shows up later as a cash problem nobody can explain.

What a draw schedule actually is

A draw schedule is the agreed plan for getting paid in stages: 20% at contract signing, 30% at rough-in, 30% at completion, and the final 20% (minus retainage) when the punch list dies. Almost every commercial job and plenty of residential ones run this way.

The schedule itself lives in your contract. The problem is what happens after signing. The work runs in your head, the schedule runs in a PDF nobody opens, and the two meet only when you notice the account is lower than it should be.

The fix is mechanical, not clever: one row per draw, per job, with five columns doing all the work.

The five columns that matter

1. Milestone. Plain language: "rough-in complete", "drywall hung", whatever your contract says. If the contract says "substantial completion", write what that means in real terms, because you will forget.

2. Contract amount for the draw. What the schedule entitles you to bill at that milestone.

3. Retainage held. Usually 5-10% withheld from every draw until final acceptance. Track it as its own number, because it compounds: by draw four you can be sitting on thousands that belong to you but live in someone else's account.

4. Status: billed or not. This is the column that pays for the whole sheet. Finished work that hasn't been invoiced is money sleeping, and on a tight month it's the difference between making payroll and not.

5. Due date and days late. Draws have payment terms like any invoice. The day a draw goes late it should be sitting at the top of your Monday list.

Here is the pattern the spreadsheet catches and memory does not: a crew finishes a milestone on a Thursday, the supervisor signs off, everyone moves to the next job, and the invoice never gets written. Two months later the job "felt profitable" but the cash never arrived, because the last two draws were never billed and retainage was never requested.

The rule that fixes it: any milestone marked finished for more than three days without a matching invoice turns red. That's it. A red row on Friday means an invoice on Friday, and an invoice on Friday means payment terms that start this month instead of next quarter.

Retainage deserves its own line at the top of your cashflow

Retainage is the sneakiest number in contracting: it's your money, you earned it, and you will get it eventually, which makes it easy to ignore and painful to need. Two habits keep it honest:

  • Total retainage held, per client, on one line. When one client is holding $9,000 and another $1,200, your follow-up energy allocates itself.
  • Retainage release date in your 12-month forecast. When that money lands matters. If it's funding your January materials orders, it should be in the forecast, not in your hopes.

Putting it next to payroll

The reason this lives in the same workbook as your timesheets and 12-month cashflow instead of a standalone file: payroll happens every week, draws land when they land. When the weekly payroll view sits next to the draw schedule, the Friday question answers itself: which invoices to send, which draws to chase, and whether next week's crew hours are already covered by money earned but not yet collected.

That linked view is exactly what we built into the Contractor Command Center: draw schedule with retainage held and net-due math, unbilled-work flags, and the cashflow that counts draw dates where they actually land. The method above works in any spreadsheet. The linked version just means you'll actually keep it current, because the payroll view guilt-trips you every Friday.

The Monday routine, condensed

  • Every finished milestone: invoice within three days, no exceptions, no batching to month-end.
  • Every billed draw: due date, days late, oldest first.
  • Retainage total per client: one glance, chase the big ones quarterly.
  • Next week's payroll against expected collections: green means fine, red means chase before Friday.

Run that for two months and the draw schedule stops being paperwork. It becomes the heartbeat: work finished, invoice out, cash in, crew paid. The way it always should have been.
