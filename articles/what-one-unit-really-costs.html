What one unit of your product really costs you, and what you earn from it

You know what you paid for your materials. You know what you charge. What sits between those two numbers is the whole question: does each sale actually pay you? Plenty of makers never find out, because costing feels like accounting. It is not. It is one page of arithmetic, and this article walks you through it. By the end you can work out what one candle, one pair of earrings or one pot costs you - and what you keep when it sells.

The receipt is not the cost

"What it cost me" is not the till receipt for your supplies. That receipt has three problems.

First, your time is not free. Every hour at the bench is an hour you are not earning anywhere else. If your business cannot pay you a real hourly rate, it is an expensive hobby. So the cost of a unit must include labour, at a rate you choose - a living wage, not a symbolic one.

Second, packaging counts. The box, the tissue, the sticker, the thank-you card: they leave with every single order, so they belong in the cost of every unit. They are small, which is exactly why they get forgotten.

Third, small consumables count. Labels, wicks, jump rings, cello bags. Individually trivial, together a real cost. If it goes into the product or out of the door with it, it goes on the sheet.

Price a small amount from what you bought

You do not buy wax by the gram or card by the sheet, so you need a way to price a pinch of something you bought by the kilo. The rule is simple: for each thing that goes into one batch, write down three numbers - how much you use per batch, how much you bought it as, and what you paid. The only trick is that "used" and "bought as" must be in the same unit: grams with grams, each with each.

Then: used divided by bought-as, times the price paid, is the cost of that material for the batch. Divide by the number of units the batch makes, and you have the cost per unit. Say you use 540 grams of soy wax per batch, and you buy wax in a 5,000-gram bag for £24.00. That is 540 / 5,000 × £24.00 = £2.59 per batch. If the batch makes six candles, the wax in each one cost about 43p. Do that for every line and the materials side is done.

Labour means the minutes you are actually working on a batch, stage by stage: weighing and melting, blending and pouring, cleaning up. Add the stages, divide by sixty, multiply by your hourly rate. That is the labour cost of the batch.

Passive time is different. Curing, drying, firing in a kiln you are not watching, proving dough overnight - none of that is labour. You are not working; the clock is. Counting passive time as labour would inflate your "cost" and push you to overprice. (If you genuinely attend the oven for an hour, that hour is work - the line is your attention, not the clock.)

Overhead without an accountant

Big firms allocate electricity, rent, insurance, equipment and software with great precision. A one-person studio does not need to. A fair starting point is a flat percentage on top of materials + labour + packaging - say 10%. Crude, yes. But far better than zero, which is the usual default.

The percentage is yours to set. Melt heavy wax or run a kiln all day? Use a higher figure. Work from a kitchen table with a secondhand burner? Lower is defensible. Some share of your tools, workspace, power and insurance sits inside every unit; a flat percentage is an honest first estimate of it.

Fees: a percentage and a fixed part

Marketplace and payment fees come in two shapes. A percentage of the price - often around or above 10% once the marketplace and the card processor have both taken theirs. And a fixed part per sale, such as a listing fee or the flat end of processing. Check the current fee pages of your platform; they change, and old numbers lie.

The two shapes hurt in different ways. The fixed fee is a flat toll: 45p is nearly nothing on an £40 order but a serious bite on a £4 one. The percentage grows with the price, which matters next.

Margin and markup are not the same thing

One paragraph, one pair of numbers. Margin is profit as a share of the price: profit ÷ price. Markup is profit as a share of the cost: profit ÷ cost. Take a unit that costs £6.32 and sells for £18.00, leaving £9.43. The margin is 9.43 / 18.00 = 52.4%. The markup is 9.43 / 6.32 = 149%. Same unit, same profit, wildly different percentages - and shops quoting "150% markup" and "52% margin" are describing identical economics. When you read your own numbers, use margin (share of the price) as your yardstick: it tells you what lands in your account per pound taken.

What the sheet tells you at the end

A one-product cost sheet boils all of this down to four outputs:

  • Cost per unit - materials + labour + packaging + overhead, before any fees.
  • Profit per unit - your price minus fees, minus any shipping you absorb, minus the cost.
  • Margin % - profit ÷ price, flagged red when it falls below the target you set.
  • Break-even price and target price - the price at which profit is zero, and the price that hits your target margin.

The last two deserve a word, because they are where naive pricing breaks. To break even you must cover the cost, the fixed fee and the absorbed shipping - and then add enough to cover the percentage fee, which grows with the price. So the formula divides rather than adds: (cost + absorbed shipping + fixed fee) ÷ (1 − fee %). For a target margin, the denominator becomes (1 − fee % − target margin). If you ignored the percentage fee you would set a "break-even" price that quietly loses money on every sale.

A worked example (illustrative numbers)

These figures are an illustration, not a real business. Imagine a hand-poured candle: a batch of six, an hourly rate of £18.00, overhead at 10%, a price of £18.00, and fees of 10% plus £0.45 per sale, with the buyer paying shipping.

  • Materials: wax £2.59 per batch (above), fragrance oil 45 g of a 500 g bottle at £18.00 = £1.62, wicks 6 of 100 at £12.00 = £0.72, jars 6 of 12 at £21.60 = £10.80, labels 6 of 100 at £15.00 = £0.90. Batch total £16.63, or £2.77 per candle.
  • Labour: 20 min weighing and melting, 15 min blending and pouring, 10 min cleaning up = 45 minutes. That is 45/60 × £18.00 = £13.50 per batch, or £2.25 per candle.
  • Packaging: box £0.55, tissue and sticker £0.12, card £0.05 = £0.72.
  • Overhead: 10% of (2.77 + 2.25 + 0.72) = £0.57.
  • Cost per unit: £6.32.
  • At £18.00: fees are 18 × 10% + 0.45 = £2.25, so profit is £9.43 and the margin is 52.4%.
  • Break-even price: (6.32 + 0.45) ÷ (1 − 0.10) = 6.77 ÷ 0.90 = £7.52.
  • Price for a 30% margin: 6.77 ÷ (1 − 0.10 − 0.30) = 6.77 ÷ 0.60 = £11.28.

Break-even is £7.52, not £6.77, because the percentage fee climbs with the price. And a genuine 30% margin needs £11.28, not the £8.80 from simply adding 30% to £6.77. Fees always take their share off the top.
