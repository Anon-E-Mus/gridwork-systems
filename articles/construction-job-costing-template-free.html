Every contractor prices jobs. It is easy to go months without knowing what they actually cost until the tax accountant tells you. A free job costing template turns that into a ten-minute-a-week check. Here is the method, the template layout, and where to get a free one right now.

What job costing actually means

Job costing is not bookkeeping. Bookkeeping tells you the business made money last year. Job costing tells you that job 14 on Maple Street ate $3,200 of unbilled labor and that your drywall sub is consistently 15 percent over quote. One looks backward, the other looks at the job you can still fix.

The core is simple: every dollar gets coded to a job before it enters the books. The materials invoice from the supplier goes to job 14. The guy who spent Tuesday on job 9 and Wednesday on job 14 has his hours split. The truck payment gets allocated across active jobs by labor hours. That one habit is the entire difference between hoping and knowing.

The four buckets (what the template tracks)

Every job carries four budget lines from the bid. This is what the template compares against actuals:

1. Labor — hours times loaded rate. Loaded means what an hour actually costs: wage plus taxes plus insurance divided by billable hours. Not what you pay the guy, what the hour costs the business.

2. Materials — at cost, then deliberately marked up 15 to 30 percent. You are being paid to source, haul, warranty and guarantee those materials. That is work and it deserves margin.

3. Subcontractors — their quotes to you, plus your coordination margin. The margin is not greed, it is insurance against their mistakes becoming your problem.

4. Overhead allocation — fuel, tool wear, insurance, the months of slow work. A flat 10 to 20 percent of job cost. Crude and vastly better than zero.

The three reports that matter once the template is running

1. Job cost report — budget vs actual per bucket, per job. The pattern that shows up every time: labor runs over on the jobs you bid optimistically, materials run over where plans changed mid-job, and overhead silently eats the jobs that dragged.

2. Work in progress (WIP) schedule — what you have spent plus what you are owed vs the contract price. This is the one banks and sureties ask for. The template calculates it automatically from the cost and invoice tabs.

3. Aging report — because in construction the gap between doing the work and getting paid is the actual business risk.

Run these monthly. The template catches problems while the job is still on. That is the whole point.
